Drop the Label: We Are All Software Engineers Now

By Pedro Santos · November 2026 · 7 min read

I am preparing a workshop for a client, and they asked me a fair question. Some of the people attending are iOS developers. Some are front-end developers. My exercises looked like back-end exercises. Would they still get something out of it?

I answered the practical question. But the question behind it stayed with me for days: are we still a profession of iOS developers, Android developers, React developers, Java back-end developers?

I don't think we are. I think that profession of labels is already gone, and many of us have not been told. The depth is not going. The label is.

Thirty years of labels

I know what it feels like to be a label, because I have worn a lot of them.

I started as a Pascal developer on DOS machines. When I started working, I moved to Unix and became a Unix engineer writing C. In the 90s I moved to the Mac and was writing Pascal again, so I was a Mac developer. Then the Mac moved to C, and so did I.

Then I went to Windows, and I was a Windows developer doing C++ and COM. Then C# arrived, and I became a .NET developer, a back-end developer. Then the iPhone happened, and I became an iOS developer writing Objective-C, building and publishing my own apps for the App Store. Then Swift. Then back to C# on the back end, as a software craftsman at Codurance in London, and later at ASOS.

Every one of them ended. And every time, what carried over was never the platform or the language. It was the things underneath: breaking a problem down, designing something that can change, testing it, reading someone else's code, talking to the people who will use it.

The labels were never the job. They were where the job happened to be at the time.

What the numbers say now

This is not only my story. The job market is saying the same thing.

Front-end and native mobile titles are shrinking fastest. The Pragmatic Engineer's 2026 analysis of job titles on LinkedIn found front-end engineer titles disappearing fastest, followed by native iOS and Android titles (The Pragmatic Engineer). The reasons given are cross-platform frameworks that remove the need for separate native teams, and AI that makes it easier for a front-end developer to take on back-end work (summary).

"Software engineer" dwarfs the specialist titles. In one sample of active postings from June 2026, software engineer roles outnumbered mobile developer roles by roughly 18 to 1: 46,486 against 2,601 (InterviewStack, an interview-prep vendor).

This started before AI. Ten years of Stack Overflow surveys show a shrinking share of developers who only do front-end or only do back-end work (Boot.dev). In the 2025 survey, full-stack was still the most common role (App Developer Magazine on the 2025 survey). AI did not start the trend. In my experience, it is speeding it up.

Java postings rarely ask for AI skills. In one sample, only 6% of Java postings asked for AI or ML skills (MisuJob). Separately, Indeed reported that software development postings rose 14% year over year in April 2026, and that 47% of them mentioned AI (Fortune). The two samples are different, but they point the same way: the growth is where AI is part of the work.

Most of these numbers come from US-heavy job boards. Even between countries the picture differs: postings rose in the UK and the US but fell in Germany and France (The Pragmatic Engineer, part 1). Read the numbers as direction, not as an exact forecast for your city. But the direction is clear.

This is grief, and that is allowed

I want to be careful here, because a lot of writing about AI and our profession is either cheerleading or doom. This is neither.

If you have spent ten or fifteen years becoming an excellent iOS developer, or the person everyone calls when the Java back end breaks, that identity is not trivial. You earned it. It came with respect, with a community, with conference talks and job offers. Hearing that the label is fading feels like hearing that the years behind it were wasted.

They were not wasted. But it is still a loss, and losses are grieved. Denial ("my stack is different"), anger ("this is just hype"), bargaining ("I'll learn one more framework and stay where I am"). I have been through every one of those, more than once, every time a platform I loved stopped being the place where the work was.

You deserve the time to feel that. People who have given this profession decades deserve honesty and respect.

What I will not do is pretend the change is not happening. That would be the unkind thing.

What the data does not say

Honesty cuts both ways, so here is what the numbers do not prove.

  • Depth is not worthless. The important decisions still need specialized knowledge: choosing an architecture, finding a hard performance or memory problem, knowing what a platform can and cannot do safely. What changes is the daily coding. With an AI agent next to you, you can write good code in a layer you know less well, and bring in the deep knowledge when a decision needs it. Depth is still valuable. It is just no longer a job title on its own.
  • Java jobs are not all legacy jobs. The market looks a lot like enterprise and legacy work, but in my experience plenty of it is modern services in the cloud.
  • "No AI in the posting" does not mean "no AI in the job." Even general software engineer postings rarely list the AI coding tools that engineers use every day (InterviewStack). Job ads lag the work.

So the claim is narrower than "specialists are finished." It is this: the specialist label is no longer a safe place to stand.

Maintainer or modernizer

Legacy systems are not going away. In my experience, banks, insurers and airlines will run Java and C# for a long time. The question is not whether that work exists. It is which role you play in it. This is how I see the two roles:

Maintainer and modernizer, as I see them
RoleMaintainerModernizer
The workKeeps the system alive, ticket by ticketUnderstands it, puts it under test, refactors it, moves it forward
What AI does to itAutomates it fastestMultiplies its value on large, poorly understood code
The skillsKnowledge of one stackCharacterization tests, safe refactoring, incremental design, directing agents
Where you standStable, with little leverageScarce and valuable

Old systems do not need old skills. They need the ones that were never about a language: reading code you did not write, getting it under test, changing it safely. With an AI agent in the loop, those skills matter more, not less. They are what turns an agent from a risk on a legacy codebase into a multiplier.

The iOS and front-end version of the maintainer is the engineer who only ever touches one layer. The modernizer is the one who can follow a feature from the screen to the database and back.

Drop the label, keep the depth

So here is my message, as plainly as I can put it.

You are not a C# back-end developer. You are not an iOS developer.

You are a software engineer who happens to know C#, or iOS, very well.

That is not a demotion. It is the thing you always were underneath the label. Some practical ways to start living it:

  • Introduce yourself differently. "Software engineer, strongest in mobile" opens doors that "iOS developer" closes.
  • Take one ticket outside your layer this month. With an AI agent next to you, the barrier is lower than it has ever been.
  • Invest in the skills that survived every platform change. Test-driven development, refactoring, design, and reading other people's code. They traveled with me through seven labels, and they are exactly what make AI-generated code trustworthy.
  • Learn to direct agents, not just use autocomplete. Specifications, tests that judge the output, and boundaries the agent cannot cross. That is where I see the work going. I describe the method in Pedro's L3 Law.

Back to that client question. Yes, the iOS and front-end developers will get something out of a back-end exercise. The practice is the same in every language and on every platform. That is the point.

I have dropped seven labels in thirty years. It hurt a little every time. It was the right call every time.
